Retrieval evaluation

Sized to the corpus

This site's assistant retrieves through typed tools over a generated content index, not through a vector database. That was a decision, so it gets an experiment rather than an assertion — four retrieval arms, one golden question set, published numbers.

Anyone can wire a vector database. Almost nobody publishes the evaluation that says they did not need one. The deliverable here is not “I chose simple”; it is “I measured retrieval against the corpus, and here is the table.” The arms are measured on the same questions with the same scoring, and the honest result is reported even where it embarrasses the design.

02

Method

Offline, free, no API key, runs in CI

The arms. tools-only navigates by name — every project id, title, client and tag is already in the assistant's system prompt, so it matches the question against that surface and reads the entity it finds. bm25 is Okapi BM25 over term statistics precomputed at build time. hybrid puts tools first and appends BM25 beneath, which was the shape originally proposed for production. tools-gated keeps BM25's ranking but lets the name match decide only whether to answer at all. embeddings is Voyage, implemented and gated behind an API key; without the key it skips and says so rather than being replaced by a cosine similarity wearing a semantic label.

Two hit rates, because they measure different failures. hit@k asks whether the right chunk came back; ent@k asks whether the right project or role did. An arm that answers “what was Domestina about?” with the Domestina research section found the right thing and chose the wrong paragraph of it, and one number should not hide that from the other.

The questions. Written as a person would ask them, and grounded in the real corpus — the runner refuses to start if any expected chunk id does not exist in the index. Roughly a fifth of them are questions the corpus cannot answer, because a retriever that never abstains is a retriever that will confidently answer anything.

What is deliberately not measured here. Citation validity in a real answer, turns per answer, latency and cost per answer all need a live model. They belong with the chat endpoint, not with the retriever. hit@k, MRR and abstention are properties of retrieval alone, so this suite is free, offline, deterministic, and runs on every commit — which is the only reason an eval suite survives contact with a real project.

Nothing here is tuned on these questions. BM25 keeps the standard k1 = 1.2, b = 0.75. The tools arm weights a matched name by its inverse document frequency in the corpus, a statistic that comes from the index rather than from the test set. Abstention is reported threshold-free: separability is the fraction of answerable/unanswerable question pairs that any score cut would order correctly, so no magic number is chosen after seeing the answers.

04

Reading the numbers

Including the parts that went badly

Plain lexical search won on ranking, and the structured arm lost. BM25 reaches 83.7% hit@3 and 0.706 MRR; navigating by name reaches 49.0% and 0.485. The design being evaluated here expected the opposite, and the number is reported the way it came out. The reason is visible in the failures: a name match fires on whichever entity happens to share a word with the question, and on questions phrased the way people actually phrase them — “what user research methods does he use?” — no entity is named at all. The corpus is small, single-author and lexically consistent, which is precisely the condition under which BM25 is strong.

Knowing when to say nothing is where they separate, and it is not close. BM25 abstains on 7 of 16 unanswerable questions. It returns a confident ranked list for “did he ever work at Google?” — the word appears eight times in the corpus, in Google Analytics, Google Play and Google AI Studio, none of them an employer — and for “what are his salary expectations?”, and for “does he speak Japanese?”. The tools arm abstains on 13 of 16, because no entity was named. That is not a tuning artefact; it is the difference between matching words and resolving identifiers.

Fusing the two rankings made both worse. Tools-first with BM25 appended scores 57.1% hit@3 — below BM25 alone — and abstains on nothing, because the fallback always fires. It inherits the weaker ranking and the weaker judgement. That result killed the originally proposed shape, which is the most useful thing this table did.

What the failure suggested instead. Stop fusing rankings and split responsibilities: let the name match decide whether the corpus covers the question, and let BM25 decide what comes back once it does. That arm — tools-gated — holds 34.7% hit@3 and 0.662 separability, tied for best in the table, while raising the abstention rate to 93.8%. Roughly seven points of ranking traded for the entire ability to say “not on file”. It is labelled post-hoc everywhere it appears, because it was written after seeing the first three arms on this same question set; it is a hypothesis this run suggests, not a result this run establishes, and it needs questions written after it to become one.

Two holes in the corpus, found by the eval rather than by a reviewer. Location, availability and contact details live in content.json as structured fields and appear in no chunk, so “where is he based?” cannot be answered by any search over prose — the only chunk containing the word “Sofia” is the education line, about Sofia University, and BM25 duly returns something else entirely. Separately, the 2007–2009 quality assurance role has no bullet points, so the content build produced no chunk for it at all: the role is in the index and invisible to search, and every arm scores zero on it. Both are why the tool layer reads structured fields directly instead of searching for them.

One question no arm could answer, and it is the case for embeddings. “How far has he run?” shares no term with “42 km. Marathon finisher.” — lexical search returns nothing at all. That is the exact shape of question a semantic retriever exists for. The embeddings arm has since been run: it answers this question and wins every retrieval class, at 91.8% hit@3 against BM25's 83.7%. The argument became a measurement.

The point the corpus statistics make on their own. The complete table of contents — every project id, title, client, tag and one-line summary, every role and span — is a few thousand characters. It fits in the system prompt. A retriever exists to solve “I cannot enumerate what is in here”; when the whole index fits in the prompt, that problem does not exist, and provisioning a stateful, billed service to solve it anyway is a decision about a résumé rather than about a corpus. That argument survives this table. The claim that structured lookup also ranks better did not.
